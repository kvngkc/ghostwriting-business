# Heritage Building Primer (for the writer)

Enough background to interview heritage contractors and avoid obvious mistakes. This is a starting vocabulary, not an authority. When a client uses a term differently, the client is right for their post. If you're unsure about a claim, mark it `[CHECK]` and ask.

## Rules that keep posts credible

- Never call lime "cement", or say "mortar" when the client said "lime mortar". In this world the difference *is* the point.
- Don't give repair advice the client didn't give ("you should repoint with X"). Specifiers read these posts, and wrong advice costs the client their reputation.
- Name a building's location or owner only if the client confirms the owner agreed.
- Use the client's own words for techniques, even if a textbook says it differently.

## Protection and process (England)

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| Listed building | A building on the national list for special architectural or historic interest. Grades: **I** (exceptional), **II\*** (particularly important), **II** (special interest, by far the most common). |
| Listed building consent | Permission needed for works that affect a listed building's character. Done without consent, such works can be a criminal offence. |
| Conservation area | An area protected for its character. Extra planning controls apply. |
| Conservation officer | Local authority officer who advises on and assesses works to listed buildings and conservation areas. A key audience. |
| Historic England | The public body for England's historic environment. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own bodies (Historic Environment Scotland, Cadw, and the Department for Communities). |
| SPAB | Society for the Protection of Ancient Buildings. Promotes "conservative repair": repair rather than replace, and keep as much original fabric as possible. |
| Like-for-like repair | Repair using the same materials and methods as the original. |
| Historic fabric | The original material of the building. |

## Materials and crafts

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| Lime mortar / lime plaster | Traditional binder that is softer and more breathable than cement, so moisture can escape. Types clients may mention: **non-hydraulic** (lime putty, hot-mixed lime) and **NHL** (natural hydraulic lime, graded NHL 2, 3.5 and 5). |
| Hot-mixed lime | Quicklime slaked with sand during mixing. A traditional method that has come back into use. |
| Repointing | Renewing the mortar joints between bricks or stones. Repointing old walls with hard cement mortar is a well-known cause of damage. |
| Breathability | The ability of traditional solid walls to absorb and release moisture. Many heritage repairs are about restoring it. |
| Ashlar / rubble | Ashlar is finely dressed, squared stone laid in even courses. Rubble is rough, uneven stone. |
| Dressing / tooling | Shaping and surface-finishing stone by hand. |
| Indent / stone replacement | Cutting out a decayed stone and inserting a new piece. |
| Leadwork | Lead roof coverings, flashings and gutters, a specialist trade with its own association. |
| Natural slate / stone slate / clay tile / thatch | Traditional roof coverings. Regional types matter: Welsh slate, Collyweston, Cotswold stone, long-straw and water reed thatch. |
| Sash window | Window with sliding panels (sashes) hung on cords and weights. Restoration often means draught-proofing and repair rather than replacement. |
| Crown glass / cylinder glass | Historic hand-made glass with ripples. |
| Limewash | Traditional breathable paint finish. |
| Timber frame | Structure of jointed timbers. Repairs use scarf joints and pegs. |

## Questions that pull good stories out of heritage clients

1. What did you find when you opened it up that nobody expected?
2. What had a previous repair got wrong, and how did you undo it?
3. Which technique on this job would most builders not know?
4. How old is the building, and what can you tell from the work itself (tool marks, materials, earlier repairs)?
5. Who trained you, and what's the one thing they drilled into you?
6. What do architects or owners most often get wrong when they first call you?
7. What would have happened to the building if nobody had done this?
8. Which apprentice on site is doing something worth showing?
